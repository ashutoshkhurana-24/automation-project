Pravita's Apartment · voice pipeline

How the house listens

Six seconds of Hinglish from a phone, and a relay closes on the other side of the flat. Between those two things sit a transcriber, a grammar that runs for nothing, a language model that is not trusted with the answer, and a hub that speaks a 1990s lighting bus. This is every step, and why each one is shaped the way it is.

The shape of it

Two roads, and the whole design is about staying on the cheap one

A spoken sentence can be understood in one of two places: on the hub, for nothing, in a tenth of a second — or by a model in another country, for a fraction of a paisa and roughly a second and a half.

Everything below is arranged around that gap. The grammar tries first and is allowed to fail freely, because falling through to the model only costs time, while a wrong local guess switches something in a room somebody is sitting in. Warm marks the parts that never leave the building; cool marks the two calls that do.

One sentence, end to end

What actually happens in nine tenths of a second

"Ashu ka fan chalu karo"

Turn on the fan in Ashu's room · spoken into a Shortcut on the phone

  1. 0.00sShortcut records 6 seconds of m4a and POSTs the raw bytes to /api/hear?who=ashu. No multipart, no form — which is why the hub needs no parser.
  2. 0.00sThe hub fires the transcriber and the audio model at the same moment. Neither waits for the other.
  3. 0.79sThe transcript lands: ashu ka fan chalu karo. Romanised Latin, because the prompt insists on it.
  4. 0.79sNot a cancel. Not a good night. Both are matched first because they cost nothing and are not addresses.
  5. 0.80sThe grammar takes it. chalu karo → on; ka dropped; ashu matches a room by unique prefix. The audio call is abandoned unread.
  6. 0.81s/do/ashu/fan/on — the same address a browser or a cron line would use.
  7. 0.85sOne WebSocket to the hub, which broadcasts on the lighting bus. The relay closes.
  8. 0.93sReply: "Fan in Ashu Room is now on." Reworded for a phone that has to read it aloud.

Stage by stage

The eight things between a voice and a relay

Each stage exists because of something that went wrong once. The notes below say which.

  1. The phone local

    A Shortcut records six seconds and posts the bytes as the request body. It also sends who — which is not decoration: cancel and good night are per speaker, so "cancel" reverses what you said, not what someone else did.

  2. The ear leaves

    The recording goes to gpt-4o-transcribe with a prompt built from the live house — every room, every circuit, and a demand for romanised Latin and digits. That prompt is load-bearing. Asked without it, the same clip comes back in Devanagari, which matches no room slug and would send every sentence to the model.

    A second call runs beside it, handing the raw audio to a model that answers with a tool call directly. It exists to have already finished when the grammar declines.

  3. Three gates, before anything else local

    Cancel and good night are matched on the raw words, ahead of the grammar and long before the model. Both cost nothing, neither is an address, and nothing in this house is named any of those words — so matching them first shadows nothing.

    nahi has to be doubled to count as a cancel. "Nahi, fan band karo" is a command with a correction in front of it, and swallowing it would be the worst kind of bug: one that eats a sentence and answers as though it acted.

  4. The free grammar local

    A dozen Hinglish verbs collapse to the action words the address grammar already has — band kar do → off, tez karo → up. Possessives, fillers and the bare tails of "kar do" are dropped. Numbers spoken as words become digits. What is left is a room, a circuit and an action.

    This is a table of verbs, never nouns. A Hindi noun mapped by name is the dangerous half: parda aliased to "curtain" matches CURTAIN ROPE in Living — which is a light sitting between two motors. Nouns are resolved by kind instead, on every road.

  5. Questions are kept out of it local

    "Is the ashu fan on" strips to [ashu, fan, on] — an impeccable command. It switched the fan on. The grammar matches three words against an address, and an address has no mood, so it cannot be taught the difference; questions are detected and handed straight to the model instead.

    The test is deliberately generous, because the two mistakes are not equal. A command mistaken for a question still works — it costs a second. A question mistaken for a command switches something on in an occupied room.

  6. The model, only when asked leaves

    It is given the house — every room and what is wired in it — and three tools: control, look, cue. Rooms are a closed list; circuits are not, because which exist depends on the room. It is explicitly told not to answer a question itself: it says what to look at, and the reading is filled in from the hub.

    Repeated sentences never reach it twice — the resolution is remembered, keyed on the words and the shape of the house, so a renamed room can't be answered from a memory of the old one.

  7. The guards local

    What comes back is a suggestion. If the sentence carried parda or pankha, the model's choice of circuit is discarded and the word is resolved locally by kind. If it answered "the whole house" for a sentence that named one kind, the target is narrowed to that kind — never widened. Word actions like full are translated to numbers the address accepts.

  8. One executor, one hub local

    Both roads end at runAddress — the same code a typed URL reaches. Rooms and circuits match on a unique prefix; two candidates is a refusal, never a guess. From there the circuits are sorted by what they physically are, because the hub does not treat them alike.

What it refuses

The interesting part is what it declines to say

Most of the care in this pipeline is spent making sure it never sounds more certain than it is.

It won't say yes about an air conditioner

Infrared is one-way. The hub blasts a code and hears nothing back, so the record is what it last sent, not what the unit is doing. Somebody using the remote is invisible to it.

"the hub last sent AC on, but can't check it"

It won't guess between two curtains

Living has a main and a sheer. A prefix that matches both is a refusal that names them, never a coin toss — the same rule that makes a mistyped address safe.

"that could be main curtain or sheer curtain — which one?"

It won't tell you where a curtain is

The motors report no position at all. Cancel reverses the verb and says plainly that this is not exactly where it started, rather than claiming a restore it can't support.

"it has no position to report"

It won't answer a kind with the whole house

"Saare AC band kar do" once switched off every light in the flat, and answered "Everything is now off." The address honours the kind now, and refuses a word it can't.

"I can do the lights, the fans, the curtains or the air conditioners"

The reply

Written to be read aloud by a phone

Everything answers HTTP 200, whatever happened. Shortcuts reads no body from a non-2xx, so a refusal sent as a 400 arrives on the phone as silence — the house sounding broken when it merely misheard. The verdict rides in the body instead.

Then the sentence is reworded for the voice: a verb added, contractions in, em dashes out (they read as a pause of unguessable length), and acronyms meant as words lowered so COBs isn't spelled out letter by letter — while AC and TV are lifted so they are.

}

heard is there for one reason: when the house does the wrong thing, the only useful question is what it thought you said.

Deliberately absent

Four things it does not do

It keeps no recording and no transcript. The audio is a buffer that is never written to disk, and the log records the road, whether it came from a microphone, and whether it worked — never the words. What leaves the house is the recording itself, going to the transcriber; a short English command typed into the dashboard never leaves at all.

It never re-reads the house to answer faster. A command trusts the background reader; only a question pays for a fresh look, and even then the poll now runs beside the model call rather than after it.

It only goes one step back. A second "cancel" says there is nothing to cancel, which is understandable — where a cancel that toggles back and forth is something nobody can keep track of by ear.

It does not switch a television off at bedtime. Good night is a sleep, and sleep leaves the fan and the air conditioner running on purpose — the point is to fall asleep, not to be woken at two by a room gone still and warm. The screens are simply not in that list, which is worth revisiting.

Verbatim

What the models are actually told

All three prompts are generated from the live house, so nothing here was written by hand — it is read back from the running server.

That distinction has already cost something. A benchmark run against a reconstructed copy of this prompt was one sentence short, and under it the model turned a question into a command in two runs of four. Against the real endpoint the same sentence answered correctly eight times out of eight. A second derivation of a prompt is worth less than no copy at all.

Rules. Pick the circuit whose slug best matches what was said; use "all" for a
whole room and room "house" for the entire home. A curtain takes only open,
close or stop — "band karo" on a curtain means close, not off. Only a tunable
light takes warmth.

A television or receiver takes on, off, toggle, up, down, mute, unmute, or a
number — which is its volume, not a brightness.

It cannot be sent an app, a channel or a source. When the request names one —
"netflix laga do", "youtube chala do", "PS5 par daal do" — do not call a tool
at all, and in particular do not switch the screen on instead: coming on
without the app is not what was asked, and the reply would not say so. Answer
in one sentence that only power and volume are wired and the app has to be
picked on the board.

A question is not a command: "kya chalu hai", "what is on", "is the fan
running", "AC chal raha hai" all want `look`, never `control`. Do not answer
the question yourself — call `look` and the reading is filled in for you.

If the request is neither a command nor a question about this house, do not
call a tool: reply with one short sentence saying you can only control and
report on the lights, fans, curtains, air conditioners and screens.

Whatever language the request is in, answer in plain English — a short,
simple sentence, because a phone reads it out loud.

Transcription hint · sent with every recording

Short home-automation commands, in Hinglish or English, spoken by an Indian speaker. Write them in romanised Latin script only — never in Devanagari or Urdu script. Spoken numbers are written as digits: chalees is 40, bees is 20, saath is 60, sattar is 70, forty is 40, sixty is 60. Rooms are a closed list; circuits are not. Which circuits exist depends on the room, and a flat schema has no way to say that — so every room’s circuits go in the prompt instead, and the validator is pick() inside the executor. A circuit the model invents is refused by the same code that refuses a mistyped URL.

Every figure here is measured against the live house on 27 August 2026. The pipeline is about 900 lines inside server.js; the grammar is checked offline by tools/say-eval.js — 42 sentences, of which 10 must fail to resolve locally, because a wrong local answer switches the wrong thing in silence. The prompts above are regenerated by tools/dump-prompts.js into docs/prompts.md.
